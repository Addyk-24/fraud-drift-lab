\title{Drift-Aware Retraining for Credit-Card Fraud Detection:\\A Chronological Comparison of Retraining Policies on IEEE-CIS}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Aditya Katkar, Member~2, Member~3, Member~4}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Department of Computer Engineering\\\todo{Institute name}\\Guide: Dr.~Kapil Vhatkar}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Fraud-detection models are usually evaluated on a single static train/test split, which hides how they behave once deployed
and fraud tactics change. We replay the IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection data (590{,}540 transactions, 26 weeks) chronologically in weekly
windows, quantify how a model that is never retrained degrades, evaluate ADWIN, DDM and a label-free PSI trigger as drift detectors, and compare no, fixed-interval,
drift-triggered and hybrid retraining on predictive performance and operational cost. A model trained once loses 13.7\% of its weekly PR-AUC over
18 deployment weeks ($p=0.039$), and a random split overstates deployment PR-AUC by about one third. Input drift is much stronger than
performance drift (adversarial AUC 0.77 to 0.97). Every policy that retrained beats never retraining (+5.7\% to +16.2\% PR-AUC, significant after
Holm correction), and retraining pays for itself unless a single retraining costs more than about \$17{,}000. Drift-triggered policies match,
but at equal retraining effort do not measurably beat, fixed-interval retraining; a sliding training window performs the same as an expanding one, so the gain
comes from recency; and the conclusions are stable under different class weighting, tuned hyper-parameters and window length.
Label delay erodes the benefit of retraining.
\end{abstract}
\begin{IEEEkeywords}
fraud detection, concept drift, model retraining, MLOps, ADWIN, DDM, LightGBM

\section{Introduction}
Card-not-present fraud evolves continuously: attackers adapt to countermeasures and legitimate customer behaviour shifts with seasons
and products. A model trained once therefore loses accuracy after deployment, a phenomenon known as concept
drift~\cite{gama2014survey}. Most published fraud-detection studies nevertheless report results on a static random split, and rarely
examine \emph{when} a deployed model should be retrained. We address this with a chronological simulation of deployment and study:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1} How much does a model trained once degrade under chronological deployment, and how optimistic is a static split?
\item \textbf{RQ2} Can ADWIN and DDM detect the degradation, and with what delay and false-alarm behaviour?
\item \textbf{RQ3} Which retraining policy gives the best performance-versus-cost trade-off?
\item \textbf{RQ4} Which features drive predictions, and does their importance shift with drift?
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Contributions.} (i) A reproducible, leakage-controlled chronological-deployment benchmark on IEEE-CIS with an explicit label-delay option;
(ii) a quantification of the gap between static-split and deployment-time performance; (iii) a cost-aware comparison of
no, fixed-interval, drift-triggered and hybrid retraining, with bootstrap confidence intervals, budget-matched comparisons and an assumption-free break-even
retraining cost; (iv) an open-source, tested pipeline (simulator, policies, cost model, TreeSHAP analysis, dashboard, continuous integration).

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Concept drift.} Gama et al.~\cite{gama2014survey} and Lu et al.~\cite{lu2019review} survey drift detection, understanding and adaptation.
DDM~\cite{gama2004ddm} monitors the online error rate and signals drift when it rises significantly above its historical minimum;
EDDM~\cite{baena2006eddm} extends it with the distance between errors; ADWIN~\cite{bifet2007adwin} keeps an adaptive window and
shrinks it when two sub-windows differ significantly. \v{Z}liobait\.{e}~\cite{zliobaite2010delayed} studies how delayed labels affect
when a change can be detected at all, which motivates our label-delay experiments.
\textbf{Dataset shift detection.} Rabanser et al.~\cite{rabanser2019failing} compare label-free shift detectors, including classifier-based
two-sample tests; our adversarial-validation AUC and PSI follow this family and require no labels.
\textbf{Fraud detection under drift.} Dal Pozzolo et al.~\cite{dalpozzolo2018} show that supervised information in fraud detection arrives
delayed, so feedback and delayed samples must be treated differently; Carcillo et al.~\cite{carcillo2018scarff} describe a scalable streaming framework for
card-fraud detection; Adebayo et al.~\cite{adebayo2023} review machine-learning and drift techniques for card fraud.
\textbf{ML operations.} Sculley et al.~\cite{sculley2015debt} identify monitoring and retraining as recurring maintenance costs of deployed
models, which is the trade-off our cost model makes explicit.
\textbf{Gap.} Prior work on fraud and drift typically proposes a detector or an adaptive learner and reports accuracy; we compare simple retraining
\emph{policies} head to head on a public benchmark, on both performance and cost, under label delay, with uncertainty estimates.

\section{Data and Problem Formulation}
We use the IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection data~\cite{ieeecis}: transaction and identity tables joined on \texttt{TransactionID}; target
\texttt{isFraud}. Only the training files are labelled, so all experiments use them. \texttt{TransactionDT} is a time offset in seconds;
we use it only for ordering and windowing. Table~\ref{tab:data} summarises the data; 12 columns with more than 90\% missing values were dropped.

\textbf{Deployment simulation.} Transactions are sorted by time and split into $W$ windows of 7 days. A model is trained on the first
$w_0=8$ windows. For each later window $w$ it scores the window using only data available before $w$; a retraining policy
then decides whether to retrain. With label delay $\delta$, a transaction becomes a training example only $\delta$ days after it occurs.
The decision threshold is chosen on the last training window (F1-optimal) and never on deployment data.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Preprocessing}
Columns with more than 90\% missing values and constant columns are dropped; numeric columns are downcast; categorical columns are
stored as categories with one global mapping (no target information). Missing values are left to the tree learners.
Highly correlated numeric features ($|r|>0.95$) are pruned using the initial training window only. Class imbalance is handled by
class weighting; Section~\ref{sec:imb} compares it with undersampling and SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote}.
\subsection{Model}
LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} is the main learner; XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} is used for comparison. Default hyper-parameters: 300 trees, learning rate 0.05, 64 leaves, column subsample 0.5, row subsample 0.8,
minimum 50 samples per leaf, L2 regularisation 1, and \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} set to the class ratio of each training set.
A random search over eight configurations plus the default, scored by PR-AUC on the last two training weeks (time-ordered, no shuffling), selected 255 leaves, learning rate 0.1, 400 trees, column subsample 0.3, at least 100 samples per leaf and L2 regularisation 10; validation PR-AUC rose from 0.599 to 0.650. The main experiments keep the default parameters; Section~\ref{sec:sens} reports the tuned model.
\subsection{Drift detection}
ADWIN and DDM from River~\cite{montiel2021river} run over two binary streams of the never-retrained model: errors on all transactions, and misses on
fraud transactions only (closer to production, where confirmed fraud arrives via chargebacks). Covariate shift is measured by the population stability index (PSI), the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic, and the AUC of an adversarial classifier separating the reference period from a later week.
\subsection{Retraining policies}
\emph{No retraining} keeps the initial model. \emph{Fixed interval} retrains every $k$ weeks ($k\in\{2,4,8\}$) on all labelled data available at that time (expanding window).
\emph{Drift-triggered} policies retrain at the start of the week after a trigger fires: ADWIN ($\delta=0.002$) or DDM on the stream of errors on all transactions,
or on misses on fraud transactions only, or a rule that fires when weekly PR-AUC falls more than 15\% below the mean of the first three weeks after the last retraining.
Because ADWIN also fires when the error \emph{decreases}, only increases count as drift. After every retraining the detector is reset, since the new model has a new error baseline,
and a cool-down of two weeks separates consecutive triggers. A \emph{hybrid} policy additionally forces a retraining when the model is older than eight weeks.
A label-free \emph{covariate} policy retrains when at least $k\in\{3,5,8\}$ of the 20 most important features exceed PSI~0.25 against the last four training weeks. All variants are compared on the same weeks as the other policies; a sliding window uses only the most recent eight weeks of labelled data.

\subsection{Cost model}
Total cost is $C=\ell\sum_{i\in\text{missed}}a_i + c_r\,N_{\text{flagged}} + c_t\,N_{\text{retrain}}$, where $a_i$ is the amount of a fraudulent transaction that the model did not flag,
$\ell$ the share of it that is lost, $c_r$ the cost of reviewing one flagged transaction and $c_t$ the cost of one retraining.
We assume $\ell=1$, $c_r=\$2$ and $c_t=\$500$ as illustrative values, not measurements, and therefore report (i) the sensitivity of the cheapest policy to $c_r$ and $c_t$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sens}) and (ii) the assumption-free \emph{break-even retraining cost}: the value of $c_t$ at which a policy costs the same as never retraining, $(C_{\text{never}}-C_{\text{policy}})/N_{\text{retrain}}$ evaluated at $c_t=0$.
\subsection{Explainability}
We explain the deployed model with exact TreeSHAP values~\cite{lundberg2017shap}, computed natively by LightGBM, on a weekly sample of 4{,}000 transactions that contains up to 1{,}000 frauds.
Global importance is the mean absolute SHAP value; we compare the first and last three deployment weeks by rank correlation and by the change in rank of individual features.

\section{Experimental Setup}
Metrics: ROC-AUC, PR-AUC (primary, because of the severe class imbalance), recall at 1\% false-positive rate, and threshold metrics (precision, recall, F1).
Metrics are computed per deployment week and averaged. Uncertainty is estimated with a moving-block bootstrap over weeks (blocks of three weeks, 5{,}000 resamples)
and paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on weekly differences, with Holm correction across policies.
All experiments use Python 3.11, LightGBM 4.7.0, XGBoost 3.2.0, scikit-learn 1.9.1, River 0.26.1, pandas 3.0.5 and NumPy 2.4.6, with random seed 42, on a laptop with a 12-core Intel Core i7-1360P and 15.7 GB of RAM.
Each retraining of the LightGBM model on up to 8--26 weeks of data takes tens of seconds; training of all eleven main policies totalled about 37 minutes.
\section{Results}
\subsection{RQ1: degradation without retraining}
Trained on the first 8 weeks and never updated, the LightGBM model's weekly PR-AUC falls from 0.546 (mean of the first three
deployment weeks) to 0.471 (last three), a 13.7\% relative drop (Spearman $\rho=-0.49$, $p=0.039$; slope $-0.006$ per week).
ROC-AUC falls from 0.892 to 0.858 (3.8\%, $\rho=-0.60$, $p=0.008$). Recall at 1\% false-positive rate (6.6\% drop) and F1 (7.1\%) also
decline but their trends are not statistically significant ($p=0.32$ and $0.22$), so we treat the degradation as real but moderate.
The evaluation protocol matters more than the drift itself: the same learner scores PR-AUC 0.721 (ROC-AUC 0.954) on a random 80/20 split,
0.526 on a temporal 80/20 split, and 0.478 when deployed once and scored on all later weeks (0.452 on the last quarter of weeks),
i.e.\ a static split overstates PR-AUC by about one third (Fig.~\ref{fig:prot}). LightGBM outperformed XGBoost under the same protocol
(mean weekly PR-AUC 0.470 vs 0.428).

\subsection{Class-imbalance handling}\label{sec:imb}
Trained on the initial window and scored on every later week, plain training reached a mean PR-AUC of 0.523, class weighting 0.498,
random undersampling (10:1) 0.517 and undersampling followed by SMOTE 0.514. Imbalance handling therefore did not help on this data.
The main experiments nevertheless use class weighting, the usual default; the simulator re-run without it (Section~\ref{sec:sens}) confirms that dropping the weights improves absolute performance by about 6\% without changing any policy conclusion.
\subsection{RQ2: drift detection}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering\safefig{drift_detection}{\columnwidth}{drift}\caption{Detector alarms against the measured degradation onset.}\end{figure}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering\safefig{psi_heatmap}{\columnwidth}{psi}\caption{PSI of the most important features over deployment time.}\end{figure}
Run on the error stream of the never-retrained model, all four detector configurations (ADWIN and DDM, on all transactions or on
fraud misses only) fire in the first two deployment weeks and repeatedly afterwards, nine to ten weeks \emph{before} the measured
degradation onset (week 18), so as raw monitors they behave mostly as false alarms. Covariate drift, by contrast, is large and monotone:
the AUC of an adversarial classifier separating the training period from week $w$ rises from 0.77 to 0.97, three of the twenty most
important features exceed PSI 0.25 by the last week, and the most shifted feature is the browser version \texttt{id\_31} (PSI 1.69).
Drift in the inputs is therefore much clearer than drift in performance, which only declines moderately (Section~RQ1).
\subsection{RQ3: retraining policies}
Table~\ref{tab:pol} compares the policies over the 18 deployment weeks. Every policy that actually retrained improves on never retraining; the 95\% block-bootstrap interval of the
mean weekly PR-AUC gain excludes zero for all ten distinct policies and all remain significant after Holm correction (adjusted $p\le0.021$, Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}). Performance rises with retraining frequency:
fixed 2-weekly retraining gives the best mean PR-AUC (0.546, +16.2\%), followed by 4-weekly (0.530, +12.8\%) and 8-weekly (0.511, +8.8\%), with diminishing returns:
at the assumed prices, moving from 8-weekly to 4-weekly retraining saves about \$17.7k of fraud and review cost for \$1.0k of extra retraining, whereas moving from 4-weekly to 2-weekly saves about \$3.9k for \$2.0k.
Drift-triggered policies sit on the same performance-versus-effort frontier as fixed intervals rather than above it. In budget-matched comparisons with an equal number of retrainings,
ADWIN on errors versus fixed 4-weekly differs by $+0.003$ PR-AUC (95\% CI $[-0.011,0.021]$), DDM on errors versus fixed 4-weekly by $-0.011$ ($[-0.035,0.008]$), the PR-AUC-drop rule versus fixed 8-weekly by $+0.007$ ($[-0.008,0.028]$),
and ADWIN with a four-week cool-down versus fixed 8-weekly by $+0.001$ ($[0.000,0.008]$):
all intervals contain zero or are negligible, so with 18 weeks of data we find no evidence that drift triggers retrain more efficiently than a well-chosen fixed interval, and no evidence that they are worse.
Three further variants are informative. A \emph{hybrid} policy that also retrains when the model is eight weeks old behaved exactly like its drift-triggered base: the detectors always fired first, so the age cap never applied.
The label-free covariate policy (PSI above 0.25 on at least $k$ of the 20 most important features) fired once for $k=3$ (week 19; +6.6\%, interval just above zero) and never for $k=5$ or $8$, so on this data PSI alone is a weak trigger even though covariate drift is large in absolute terms.
Finally, a sliding window of eight weeks matched the expanding window (0.5305 vs 0.5304, difference $+0.0001$, 95\% CI $[-0.008,0.007]$): the benefit of retraining comes from recency, not from having more data.
Under the default cost assumptions all retraining policies are cheaper than never retraining (by 3.7\% to 16.2\%); the saving comes from fraud losses, not from retraining effort. The break-even retraining cost is
at least \$17k for every policy against an assumed \$500, so retraining pays off under any plausible price. Label delay erodes the benefit:
for 4-weekly retraining the mean PR-AUC falls from 0.530 (no delay) to 0.516 (7 days) and 0.496 (14 days, $p=0.060$ against never retraining).
Results come from a single run on one dataset, so differences of a few percent between policies should not be over-interpreted.

\subsection{Sensitivity analysis}\label{sec:sens}
We re-ran the never-retrained and fixed 4-weekly policies, and ADWIN, under alternative design choices, each against its own never-retrained baseline (Table~\ref{tab:sens}, Fig.~\ref{fig:extra}).
\emph{Without class weights} the absolute performance improves (never retrained 0.470 to 0.498, 4-weekly 0.530 to 0.565) and the relative gain of retraining is unchanged (+13.6\% vs +12.8\%).
\emph{With tuned hyper-parameters} the never-retrained model improves by 3.8\% (0.488) and the 4-weekly policy by 5.8\% (0.561 vs 0.530); the tuned model's selection gain on the validation weeks (0.599 to 0.650) is therefore partly optimistic, as it was chosen as the best of nine configurations.
Its PR-AUC gain from retraining rises to +15.0\%, yet its total cost under 4-weekly retraining is slightly \emph{higher} (\$1.42M vs \$1.40M), because cost is evaluated at one F1-optimal operating point that PR-AUC does not capture.
With \emph{14-day windows} (initial training 4 windows) the conclusions hold: 4-weekly retraining improves PR-AUC by 12.0\% (0.515 vs 0.460), and ADWIN produced results identical to fixed retraining because it fired every time its two-window cool-down allowed.

\subsection{RQ4: explainability}
Exact TreeSHAP values of the deployed model show a \emph{stable} reliance on the same signals: the importance ranking of the first and
last three deployment weeks is almost identical (Spearman $\rho=0.995$, 9 of the top-10 features shared). The notable exceptions are the
identity strings \texttt{id\_31} and \texttt{id\_30} (browser and operating-system versions), which lose 12 and 10 rank positions. They are also
the most shifted features by PSI (\texttt{id\_31}: 1.69), which suggests that new browser/OS versions unseen in training erode
a useful signal rather than the model's logic changing. The features that gain rank (\texttt{D11}, \texttt{id\_02}, \texttt{M3}) are anonymised by the dataset owner, so we do not attempt a business interpretation.


\section{Discussion}
\textbf{Input drift is not the same as performance drift.} The inputs drift strongly (adversarial AUC 0.97 by the last week) while PR-AUC declines only moderately and the model's feature
reliance is stable. A monitor based on input shift alone would therefore fire long before retraining is worth its cost, and error-based detectors fire too early on a never-retrained
model with only 3.5\% positives. This explains why drift-triggered policies did not improve on fixed schedules in our budget-matched comparisons.
\textbf{Practical guidance.} A fixed interval of about four weeks is a strong, simple default: it captured most of the gain of 2-weekly retraining at half the retraining effort.
Drift monitors are most useful as a safety net for abrupt changes, for instance in a hybrid policy with a maximum model age.
\textbf{Label delay matters.} Fraud labels arrive late; at a 14-day delay the benefit of 4-weekly retraining shrinks from +12.8\% to +5.6\% and is no longer significant at the 5\% level, so retraining schedules should be set with the labelling delay in mind.
\textbf{Retraining adds data as well as recency.} With an expanding window a retrained model sees more data \emph{and} more recent data; part of the gain may be the former.
The sliding-window experiment separates the two: an eight-week sliding window performed the same as the expanding window, so the benefit comes from recency.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\emph{External:} a single dataset covering six months, without calendar dates, so seasonality cannot be identified; the features are anonymised, which limits interpretation. \emph{Internal:} one model family and one random seed, so no variance over training randomness is reported;
the decision threshold is tuned on one week; detector parameters were fixed a priori but may favour one method; the cost constants are assumptions, which is why we report sensitivity and break-even values; hyper-parameters were chosen as the best of nine configurations on two validation weeks; 18 deployment weeks give limited power to separate policies
whose gains differ by a few percent; the early deployment weeks follow the training period closely and the series is autocorrelated, which we address with block bootstrap but not eliminate.
\emph{Construct:} PR-AUC averaged over weeks weights each week equally regardless of its fraud count.

\section{Conclusion}
A fraud model trained once degrades measurably but moderately under chronological deployment (13.7\% lower weekly PR-AUC after 18 weeks), and static splits overstate its performance by about one third (RQ1).
Raw ADWIN and DDM alarms on the error stream are early and frequent, whereas covariate drift is large and monotone, so detector alarms alone are a poor guide (RQ2).
Every policy that retrained beats never retraining and pays for itself at any plausible retraining price; the more frequent the retraining the better, with diminishing returns, and drift-triggered policies match but do not measurably beat fixed intervals at equal effort. A sliding window performs like an expanding one, so the gain comes from recency, and the conclusions are stable under different class weighting, tuned hyper-parameters and window length (RQ3).
The deployed model relies on the same features throughout, except browser and operating-system strings whose values drift (RQ4).
